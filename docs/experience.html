Experience

There's something remarkable about watching an idea become real.

I've had the chance to experience that from almost every angle: writing the first lines of code, building the systems that businesses depend on, and bringing together the people who make it all possible.

Some of the most rewarding moments come from the unexpected. A difficult problem that demands a different approach. Someone on the team seeing a possibility nobody else had considered. Watching people accomplish something together that none of them could have done alone.

Agentic AI brings that same sense of possibility. I've developed structured agent workflows and seen firsthand what they can accomplish, along with the engineering discipline needed to make them dependable. The opportunity goes far beyond generating code. It's about connecting agents to real systems, giving them meaningful work, and building the safeguards that let people trust the results.

I've learned what it takes to turn ideas into reality. What excites me is what we haven't built yet, and the people I'll get to build it with.

Learning to Own the Whole Problem

Shadow Technologies | Software Engineering & Executive Leadership | 2000-2009

Co-founding Shadow Technologies gave me the opportunity to experience technology from nearly every angle, well beyond writing software.

Most of our work involved helping customers solve problems through custom software, web applications, networking, and infrastructure. I designed and developed software, architected and managed hosting infrastructure, worked directly with customers, and handled everything from sales to support and business operations.

Customers often came to us with a solution already in mind. I found that some of the most valuable conversations came from understanding what they were really trying to accomplish. By working through the possibilities together, we could shape projects around their goals, sometimes finding simpler or more effective approaches than originally envisioned.

We also took on the development and operation of an existing online game. We rewrote substantial portions of the software, built a community around it, and experimented with monetization. It was a different experience from our customer projects, where we had to think not only about the technology, but also about the people using it, keeping them engaged, and sustaining the product.

What I took from Shadow Technologies was an appreciation for the whole picture. Engineering wasn't just about delivering working software. It was about understanding people, making sound decisions, managing resources, and creating something that genuinely served its purpose.

I carried that perspective into Clarity, where the systems I helped build would become central to how an entire business operated.

When the Software Becomes the Business

Clarity Voice · Platform Engineering & Executive Leadership · 2006–2020

A customer takes a new phone out of the box, plugs it in, and expects it to work.

Simple enough.

But behind that expectation is an entire world of technology. Equipment provisioning, switching platforms, carriers, billing, monitoring, customer management, and all the infrastructure holding it together.

At Clarity Voice, I had the opportunity to build much of that world.

Building the Platform

Some of our systems were inherited and needed significant improvement. Others we designed and built from the ground up.

I personally developed the telecommunications rating and billing platform that supported the business. It processed call records, managed service plans, calculated charges and prorations, generated invoices, handled payments, and tied everything together with customer management and CRM functionality.

The platform became central to the company's operations and remains in use today.

I also built systems that automated the provisioning of customer equipment and telephone services. Phones from multiple manufacturers could be ordered through a distributor and automatically configured when customers connected them for the first time.

On the switching side, I developed integrations across multiple telecommunications platforms, including automated customer provisioning and migrations.

One particularly interesting challenge was upgrading switching infrastructure without forcing all customers to move at once. I built a process that allowed individual customers to be migrated between clusters and software versions, with scheduled execution and rollback capabilities.

We could plan upgrades weeks ahead and move customers through a controlled process rather than treating every upgrade as one large event.

Keeping It Working

Telecommunications presents a different set of engineering challenges from a typical business application.

We were dealing with real-time communication systems and four-nines availability objectives. Problems weren't always easy to reproduce, and interruptions could directly affect customers.

I developed an automated testing platform using Elixir and portions of a SIP implementation to perform live testing against our switching infrastructure. I also built monitoring and troubleshooting integrations, worked through carrier interoperability, and developed routing and messaging capabilities.

Sometimes the limitations were in systems we didn't control. When vendor solutions couldn't meet our needs quickly enough, we had to understand the problem deeply enough to develop our own solutions or integrations.

It was work that demanded an understanding of the entire system, from software and networking to the behavior of external platforms.

Building the People Behind It

As the technology grew, so did the need for an engineering organization capable of developing and maintaining it.

I built the engineering team from the ground up. Rather than relying exclusively on experienced engineers, I frequently hired people with potential and invested in helping them develop.

Mentoring became one of the most rewarding parts of my career. There's something satisfying about watching someone develop the confidence and technical judgment to take on challenges that once seemed beyond their reach.

Over time, I managed engineers working locally, remotely, nearshore, and offshore. We developed testing practices, engineering workflows, and metrics that gave us better visibility into quality, progress, and delivery.

Clarity is where I came to appreciate just how valuable measurement and clear processes can be—not as bureaucracy, but as tools that help people work effectively.

My responsibilities eventually grew into the CTO role, encompassing technical strategy, architecture, infrastructure, vendor relationships, budgets, and the technology supporting daily business operations.

We also began exploring machine learning and AI concepts, considering where those technologies might eventually provide practical value.

What started as building software became something much larger: building the systems, teams, and practices that allowed a business to operate and grow.

Engineering Leadership and Modernization

Intrepid Control Systems · Senior Software Architect / Wireless neoVI Team Manager
June 2020 – September 2026

During my six years at Intrepid, my focus extended beyond developing software. I worked to improve how we designed systems, operated infrastructure, organized engineering work, and delivered products.

My responsibilities combined technical architecture, engineering leadership, and hands-on problem-solving. I managed the Wireless neoVI engineering team, participated in hiring approximately a dozen engineers, and worked closely with development and operations teams to improve the reliability and effectiveness of our connected-vehicle data platforms.

I approached these challenges through long-term engineering initiatives rather than isolated projects. Each initiative started with a specific problem, a reason it mattered to the business, and a technical direction we could work toward over multiple releases.

Connected-Vehicle Product Engineering

Alongside the platform and modernization initiatives, I had direct product accountability for neoVI RED 2, neoVI FIRE 3, and Wireless neoVI. Together, these products connected in-vehicle data collection with remote device management, data transfer, and cloud-based processing. My role required understanding how the software on the devices, the communication interfaces, and the backend services worked together.

I worked closely with engineers on client–server interface contracts, feature architecture, failure scenarios, and the design of Linux-based client software. My team developed and maintained the Linux-side software and integrations used by RED 2 and FIRE 3, with related technology also supporting neoVI Connect and the FIRE 3 portion of FIRE 3 COMPUTE. My contribution to the client software was primarily architecture, technical direction, and mentoring—not writing its C++ implementation.

During my final months, I became more directly involved in the Linux runtime, reviewing service configurations and startup behavior, investigating device-level observability, improving graceful shutdown handling to reduce the risk of data loss or corruption, and making targeted changes to the service bridging Linux and device firmware. I also personally developed the server-side integration for ValueLOG, a separate logging product whose hardware was outside my ownership.

Establishing Reliability

One of my earliest priorities was addressing recurring stability problems.

Coming from a communications environment where downtime had immediate consequences, I brought a strong emphasis on reliability. But I also recognized that Intrepid's operational requirements were different.

Working alongside operations, we established more appropriate service objectives, improved escalation procedures, standardized environments, and addressed underlying causes of recurring failures.

Containerization, resource management, and improved recovery mechanisms helped create a more dependable foundation.

Within the first several months, we had addressed most of the recurring stability problems, allowing us to shift more attention toward longer-term architectural improvements.

Evolving the Architecture

With reliability improving, I began pushing several broader modernization initiatives, including infrastructure as code, security, cloud-native architecture, and more consistent deployment practices.

One of the largest initiatives involved moving from heavily provisioned virtual machines and physical servers toward infrastructure that could scale with actual processing demand.

This required more than deploying Kubernetes.

Our processing architecture included large services that managed substantial workloads internally. Those services were difficult to scale independently because individual processing jobs shared the resources and lifecycle of their parent processes.

We began introducing resource constraints and designed a path toward independently managed, short-lived processing jobs.

We also developed a data service that abstracted file access behind an API. This provided a foundation for separating applications from their underlying storage infrastructure and eventually adopting object storage. Full adoption by data-processing jobs and the storage migration remained unfinished.

These architectural changes were motivated by both technical and financial considerations.

I treated infrastructure expense as an engineering responsibility. Through optimization efforts, we achieved approximately a 53% reduction in cloud costs from previous peak spending.

The broader cloud-native initiative offered another substantial opportunity for expense reduction through more efficient resource allocation, autoscaling, and storage modernization.

By the time I left, Kubernetes development environments were operational, automated deployments were working, and several architectural foundations had been established. The complete production migration and its projected additional savings remained future work.

Improving Engineering Workflows

Architecture wasn't the only system I wanted to improve. I was equally interested in how engineers worked.

I believe engineering processes should make developers more effective rather than introduce unnecessary bureaucracy.

We improved our GitLab CI/CD workflows so that changes merged into the master branch automatically deployed to our alpha environment and a Kubernetes development environment.

This created a more consistent delivery process and helped expose integration problems earlier.

The next planned improvement was introducing isolated review environments for merge requests. Our analysis suggested that eliminating repetitive environment preparation could save hours of engineering effort per review.

I also began exploring how AI-assisted development and automated validation could strengthen our workflows.

The direction was to establish clearer issue requirements, expand deterministic quality checks, and introduce independent automated reviews within the delivery pipeline.

My goal was to make engineering consistency a property of the system rather than something dependent entirely on individual discipline.

Observability and Continuous Improvement

Another important initiative focused on observability.

We expanded our ability to understand system behavior through technologies including Grafana and Loki, improving the foundation for monitoring, troubleshooting, and operational analysis. The broader observability initiative reached its intended release window near my departure, but I did not verify its final rollout.

But I wanted to connect that information directly to engineering improvement.

One of my final experiments involved an automated feedback loop that examined production errors, identified whether corresponding GitLab issues already existed, and created new issues when necessary.

The prototype was partially working, although I hadn't completed its validation before my departure.

The broader objective was to create a continuous relationship between production operations and development.

Instead of relying entirely on engineers to discover, investigate, and document problems manually, I wanted our tooling to surface actionable information and help drive the next cycle of engineering improvements.

My Approach to Leadership

What connected these initiatives was a consistent philosophy.

I believe effective engineering leadership requires understanding the underlying problems, establishing a clear direction, and giving teams the structure and autonomy to make meaningful progress.

I stayed technically involved while working to improve the processes and systems around the developers.

I wasn't interested in modernizing infrastructure simply because newer technology existed. Each initiative needed to address an operational problem, improve engineering effectiveness, reduce expenses, or create a measurable business advantage.

My objective was to build an engineering organization capable of delivering reliable software while continually improving how that software was designed, developed, and operated.

What's Next

Software engineering is entering a fundamental transition. AI is moving beyond assisting individual developers toward agentic workflows that can participate across the engineering lifecycle.

I've been actively exploring that transition, developing structured agent workflows that combine autonomous capabilities with deterministic validation, clear accountability, observability, and human oversight. The opportunity isn't simply to write code faster. It's to rethink how software is designed, developed, tested, deployed, and maintained.

That's where I want to focus the next chapter of my career: leading engineering teams and organizations through this evolution. I want to bring together hands-on architecture, practical AI adoption, and the engineering discipline needed to make these systems dependable and valuable to the business.

I'm interested in helping define how the next generation of software engineering actually works.
